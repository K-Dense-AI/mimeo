# Practical Heuristics & Rules of Thumb

Daily rules of thumb applied by Linda Griffith in bioengineering design and scientific research.

## Question Complex Microfluidics Before Building
Always evaluate whether a simpler biological tool or static 3D culture exists before committing to designing or building a complex microfluidic chip.

> "We always ask is there an easier way to do this? We always ask is there a simpler thing than this very complicated micrfluidic device? And if there is we're not going to do the micrfluidic device as cute as it looks."

(sources: src_008, src_009)

## Validate Stiffness Effects Orthogonally
Never infer physical matrix stiffness causation from correlation without testing an orthogonal material chemistry to rule out chemical crosslinker artifacts.

> "I really really worry in everything that we're inferring causation from correlation"

(sources: src_009, src_012)

## Prioritize Human Data for Immunological Targets
When evaluating immunologically focused drug targets, prioritize human bioengineered multi-cellular models over rodent models.

> "immunology is very species specific... when you get to the particulars, particularly how an immunologically focused drug against inflammation or other things works, you often really need to have data in humans..."

(sources: src_004, src_009, src_016)

## Replace Matrigel for Multi-Week Studies
Swap mouse-derived Matrigel for defined synthetic hydrogels whenever tissue cultures degrade or lose reproducibility in multi-week experiments.

> "We need to replace it with something that gives us better biology and better reproducibility."

(sources: src_009, src_012, src_016)

## Accept Tedious Incremental Rigor
Recognize that solid scientific progress comes from tedious, small, highly rigorous pieces put together rather than single sudden media breakthroughs.

> "Science is often incremental. I know journalists always want the breakthrough, but it's a series of - it's - it can be very, very tedious to get the science right."

(sources: src_004)

## Stay in Your Lane While Monitoring Adjacent Lanes
Maintain intense focus on your specialized engineering domain while actively keeping track of innovations in surrounding disciplines.

> "So what I believe firmly in is something called staying in your lane and keeping track of what's going on in the other lanes because you know you just never know."

(sources: src_009)
